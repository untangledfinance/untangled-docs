# How Prime works

Prime has a small number of parts. Once you know what each one does, the rest of
the docs are about using them.

| Part | What it does | Where you manage it |
|---|---|---|
| The account | Carries the authority over what the account can do | Accounts |
| Signers and approvals | Who can act, and how many must agree | Policies, Signers tab |
| Policies | Bound a delegated key to what it may do | Policies |
| Co-signing | Collect the approvals a transaction needs | Transactions |

## The account

A Prime Account is a contract on Stellar that carries the authority over what an
account can do, not the custody of its funds - your funds stay in your own
custody wallet, and Prime acts on them under the rules you set. The account is
deployed from your wallet, and its address is derived from that wallet, so the
same wallet always resolves to the same Prime Account. Deploying it a second time
binds the account that already exists instead of creating another.

Your own wallet is the account's first signer. Everything else - other signers,
approval thresholds, delegated keys - you add on top.

## Signers and approvals

Signers are the wallets allowed to act on the account. An approval threshold
says how many of them must agree before a transaction goes through: one of three,
two of three, and so on.

A threshold is what makes an account shared. Set it to two, add three signers,
and any single signer can start a transaction but it does not execute until a
second one approves.

See [Signers and approvals](./04.signers-and-approvals.md).

## Policies

A policy is a rule the account checks before it authorises a call. It bounds a
key to a specific contract, method, amount, or set of recipients. A key placed
under a policy can act only within that rule.

Policies are what let you delegate. You can give an agent a key that may claim
yield but never withdraw, or trade but never at a bad price, and the account
enforces it for you.

See [Policies](./05.policies.md).

## Co-signing

When a transaction needs more approvals than the wallet in front of you can give
alone, Prime does not fail. It produces a co-signing link. Each other signer
opens the link, sees what is being signed, and adds their signature until the
threshold is met.

What a co-signer sees is read from the transaction itself and from a fresh look
at the chain, not from anything the link claims about itself. A link cannot talk
a signer into approving a different call than the one it carries.

See [Transactions and co-signing](./06.transactions-and-co-signing.md).

## How they fit together

A transaction on Prime is authorised when it satisfies the account's rules: the
right signers have approved, and every call is within the policies that cover it.
Co-signing is how those approvals are collected when they come from more than one
wallet.
