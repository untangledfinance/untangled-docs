# Security

Prime is built so that funds move only under rules you set. This page covers what
those rules protect, what they do not, and the parts to know before you rely on
them.

## What protects your funds

- **The threshold:** a transaction executes only when the number of signers you
  set have approved it. No single signer can move funds on a shared account
  alone.
- **Policies:** a delegated key acts only within the rule it sits under. A call
  outside the rule is refused by the account, and the funds do not move.
- **Local review:** what you sign, and what a co-signer sees, is read from the
  transaction and from a fresh look at the chain. A link or a connected app
  cannot present one call and carry another.
- **Custody stays yours:** when an agent puts custody funds to work, the funds
  stay in your own custody wallet behind gates you own and can revoke. Prime's
  rule can only refuse a move, never widen what your own limits allow. See
  [Custody-preserving execution](./08.custody-preserving-execution.md).

## What a policy does not protect

These limits carry real consequences for a delegated key. Each is exercised with
a worked example, including a recording of both the permit and the refusal, under
[Worked examples](./05.policies.md#worked-examples).

- **A key is only as constrained as the loosest rule it sits on.** The caller
  chooses which rule authorises a call. If the same key is also a signer on a
  rule with no policy, it can name that rule instead and the policy never
  applies. Put a delegated key on the policed rule and nowhere else.
- **Adding a second signer does not create dual approval.** On a rule that
  carries a policy, any one signer can act alone. Adding a second signer gives
  you the opposite of two approvals. Use a threshold policy for that - supported
  on chain, but not yet offered in the app.
- **The account owner is not constrained.** A policy bounds the keys you place
  under it. Your own admin authority keeps full control, which is what lets you
  revoke.
- **A total-spend cap counts ledgers, not clock time.** A "per day" cap is a cap
  per a fixed number of ledgers. Stellar closes a ledger in roughly five seconds,
  so the period is approximate and the chain enforces the ledger count.
- **A total-spend cap only meters transfers.** The spending limit reads the
  amount from a call named `transfer`. It cannot see value moved by a method
  with another name.
- **Changing the signers stops the rule working.** If you add or remove a signer
  on a rule that carries a policy, the policy notices and starts refusing rather
  than silently applying to a different set of people than you intended.
  Reinstall the rule after changing signers.
- **Call frequency cannot be bounded.** A spend cap bounds how much, not how
  often. A key capped at 100 a day can still make a thousand calls of 0.1. We
  have opened an issue with OpenZeppelin
  [here](https://github.com/OpenZeppelin/stellar-contracts/issues/858).

## Third-party components

Total-spend caps and threshold approvals are OpenZeppelin example contracts. We
built them from OpenZeppelin's published source and deployed them ourselves. We
have not audited them, and OpenZeppelin describes that code as experimental
software provided "as is". Anything relying on them inherits that.

The policy interpreter - the part that evaluates the rule you wrote - is ours.
Its addresses and build hash are published in the repository so you can check
that the account is running the code we say it is.

## Before you rely on Prime

:::caution
Prime is under active development. Confirm which network you are on before you
commit significant value, and start with amounts you are prepared to lose while
the product matures.
:::
